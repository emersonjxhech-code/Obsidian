# Section 18.1 Review
1. The Estates-General was a French version of the English Parliament. It was very weak and simply did not meet again after the late 1400s. The real power belonged to the French king.
2. Popes had little or no power over French kings. The king practically held religious power as well as political power. He even acquired the power to appoint the bishops of the Roman church in France.
3. The idea was that the king was the same as the state, and the state was the same as the church.
4. The Huguenots were French Calvinist **Protestants**. They were persecuted, and rejected in France. By the mid-1500s, nearly half of the French nobles had become Protestant.
5. Catherine de' Medici attempted to rule for her two sons. She was a ruthless and unscrupulous ruler who used the royal power for her own selfish interests.
6. Nine civil wars were fought in France in the last forty years of the 16th century. Admiral Gaspard de Coligny led the Protestant forces, while Henry of Bourbon (later Henry IV) was a key Protestant leader. The prominent Guise family led the Catholic forces.
7. As many as 100,000 Huguenots were slaughtered during the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre. Henry of Bourbon escaped death by renouncing his Protestant faith and declaring himself a Catholic. Later, however, he defected again to Protestantism.
8. The first Protestant king of France was Henry IV.
9. The **Edict of Nantes** tolerated the Huguenots and gave them some freedom to practice their faith. It also gave them military protection.
10. Marie de' Medici attempted to rule after her husband's assassination but Cardinal Richelieu really ruled instead.
11. In the Peace of Alais, the Huguenots lost their fortified towns and armies.
# Section 18.2 Review
1. Louis XIV was four years old when his father died. While he was still a child, **Cardinal Mazarin** was appointed to rule for him.
2. During the reign of Louis XIV, France became one of the leading world powers of its time. The hardworking French people contributed to the country's international commerce, making it a very prosperous nation due to its large population, industrious people, and thriving trade. 
3. Louis XIV was known for his display of pomp and splendor. He is known as **the Sun King**.
4. Absolutism is the unlimited rule of a government by one individual. *"L'état, c'est moi"* expressed his belief in absolutism. He put the government on a religious foundation and claimed that his absolute power was by divine right.
5. The **Palace of Versailles** was an extravagant and complex building. Having achieved status as one of the great works of architecture in the Modern Age, Versailles mainly housed many government offices but with its splendid Hall of Mirrors, tapestry, chandeliers, and frequent formal occasions, it had much to keep the aristocracy occupied.
6. Louis set up a definite military organization in France with himself at the top and only allowed armed persons in France to fight for him. The French government took all charge of recruiting and training troops, and increased the size of the military from 100,000 to 400,000.
7. The tax burden in France fell upon the peasants. Because of a certain agreement with the king, the nobility did not have to pay taxes, and the bourgeoisie, or middle class, was able to escape taxation by certain exemptions.
8. The revocation of the **Edict of Nantes** ordered all Huguenot church buildings to be destroyed. It also banned Huguenot church services, exiled ministers on penalty of death, forced children born to Huguenot parents to be raised Catholic, and forbade non-minister Huguenots from leaving the country.
9. The **Age of Enlightenment** began in 1715, at the end of the reign of **Louis XIV**. Chief thinkers such as **Voltaire**, **Montesquieu**, and **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** promoted modern political ideas in France. The era was characterized by a rejection of the Bible and disbelief in the deity of Christ.
10. Modern political ideas in America have worked because they have been strengthened or restrained by the Christian heritage of the people. They would not work in France because Biblical Christianity had been driven out of the country with the Huguenots. The persecution of the Huguenots laid the foundation for the French Revolution.
# Section 18.3 Review
1. Louis XV was a weak ruler who was more interested in personal affairs than governing a nation. "After me, the deluge."
2. First Estate—Clergy, Second Estate—Nobility, Third Estate—Everyone else (which included roughly 80% of the population).
3. Facing a financial crisis, the King was forced to call the Estates-General into session for the first time in over 170 years. This action by the King helped initiate the French Revolution.
4. On July 17, 1789, the Third Estate declared itself to be the **National Assembly.** King Louis XVI responded by ordering the members of the Estates-General out of the meeting hall.
5. **Marie Antoinette** was the wife of King Louis XVI.
6. On **July 14, 1789**, the people of Paris stormed the **Bastille**. In defense, troops killed 98 people. In retaliation, several government officials were murdered.
7. The name of the French Declaration was the ***Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen***. The slogan of France and Western Europe became **"Liberty, Equality, Fraternity."**
# Section 18.4 Review
1. King Louis XVI and his family were forced to move to Paris by a mob of women, where they could be more closely watched. 
2. The **Jacobin Club** wanted the Revolution to be more extreme.
3. France's first constitution was called the **Constitution of 1791.** It gave France a constitutional monarchy with a very weak king.
4. In June 1791, Louis attempted to flee from France and get aid from foreign powers to quell the revolution in France and restore the old monarchy. He was intercepted and sent back.
5. The **Right** sat on the right while the **Left** sat on the left.
6. Key radical leaders included **Jean-Paul Marat**, **Georges Jacques Danton**, and **Maximilien Robespierre**. 
7. The **Reign of Terror** was a period in France when thousands of known and suspected "enemies of the republic" were suppressed and many were guillotined. It was Robespierre's attempt to suppress anti-revolutionary movements.
8. In an effort to de-Christianize France, the group introduced a new calendar, which removed Sundays and all religious holidays, declared the year 1792 to be **Year One**, renamed months, and converted weeks into ten days instead of seven. 
9. The French Revolution demonstrates that embracing atheism contributed to its failure. 
# Section 18.5 Review
1. **Napoleon Bonaparte** distinguished himself as a military leader during the French Revolution. Later, he threatened British India through his invasion of Egypt (1798).
2. Napoleon was declared Emperor of France by the Pope, but during the coronation ceremony, he crowned himself to symbolize his independence from the Church.
3. At the **Battle of Trafalgar (1805)**, the British navy, led by **Lord Nelson**, defeated the combined French and Spanish navies.
4. At the height of his empire, Napoleon controlled virtually all of continental Europe.
5. The Continental System was a law which forbade the importation of British goods into any European country under French influence. It failed because Europe could not get along without British imports. 
6. Czar Alexander I of Russia refused to comply with Napoleon’s Continental System. In retaliation, Napoleon invaded Russia in 1812, intending to conquer the czar and make Russia part of his empire. 
7. It has been said that the Russian winter was Napoleon's worst enemy. The harsh winter forced his army to retreat back to France, and he lost approximately 300,000 men in this endeavor.
8. The **Battle of Leipzig** is also known as the **Battle of Nations**. More troops engaged in the Battle of Leipzig than in any other battle of history until the 20th century.
9. After Napoleon was exiled, **Louis XVIII** was crowned king of France.
# Chapter 18.6 Review
1. The purpose of the **Congress of Vienna (1814–1815)** was to restore stability to Europe by redrawing national borders and establishing a balance of power. The key diplomats there were **Czar Alexander I** of Russia, **Lord Castlereagh** of Great Britain, **Talleyrand** of France, and **Prince von Metternich** of Austria.
2. The most influential man at the **Congress of Vienna** was **Prince von Metternich**. He was called the ***Prince of Diplomats***. 
3. The old monarchies of Europe were restored, except for the Holy Roman Empire, which Napoleon had reorganized into a coalition of states called the **German Confederation**. The Austrian Netherlands became part of Holland, Savoy was enlarged, and Prussia was given land along the Rhine River.
4. After Napoleon, the three monarchs who came to the throne of France were **Louis XVIII**, **Charles X**, and **Louis-Philippe**. **Louis XVIII** tried to take a moderate course between a strong monarchy and a democracy, though his policies often pleased neither side. **Charles X** favored a strong monarchy. **Louis-Philippe** was a constitutional monarch who identified with the upper middle class.
5. The immediate cause of the Franco-Prussian War was the question of who would succeed the Spanish throne. The war raged from 1870 to 1871. During the war, the chancellor of Prussia was **Otto von Bismarck**.

## Terms
1. Estates-General—French version of Parliament 
2. synod—An assembly of representatives
3. Edict of January (1562)—Allowed Huguenots to worship publicly outside town limits, provided they admitted royal officials to inspect their services.
4. **Edict of Nantes**—Commanded that Huguenots could live in any towns or districts of France they chose, but could practice their worship only in specifically designed towns where Protestantism had previously been the prevailing form of worship.
5. Peace of Alais (1629)—Ended the Huguenot rebellions by forcing them to disband their armies and surrender fortified towns, though they retained limited religious freedoms under the Edict of Nantes.
6. bourgeoisie—The French term for the Middle Class
7. League of Augsburg—A European league of nations against France
8. old regime—The system of government and way of life in pre-Revolutionary France
9. First Estate—clergy, Second Estate—nobility, Third Estate—Everyone else; included merchants and poor peasants
10. National Assembly—The official representative body of all the people of France
11. Tennis Court Oath—An oath made by the National Assembly which vowed to continue meeting until a national constitution had been written
12. August 4 Decrees—Decrees in which the National Assembly ended the lords' privileges of collecting land taxes from the peasants, hunting on peasants' lands, and taking special privileges over peasants in courts of law
13. Jacobin Club—Radical group which wanted the French Revolution to go to extremes
14. Right, Center, Left—Political factions in the National Assembly, named for the side of the chamber on which they sat.
15. National Convention—Replaced the National Assembly; was to rule temporarily and write another new constitution
16. Consulate—The new republic announced by Napoleon and his followers
17. Continental System—Enactment which forbade the importation of British goods into any European country which was under French influence
18. multiparty system—System where a nation has more than two main political parties
## Places
1. Palace of Versailles—One of the great works of architecture in the Modern Age which housed many government offices and kept the aristocracy occupied.
2. Elba—The island to which Napoleon was first exiled
3. St. Helena—The second island to which Napoleon was exiled; where he lived the remaining six years of his life
## Events
1. St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre—The mass slaughter of Huguenots which began on August 24, 1572
2. Treaty of Ryswick—Treaty ending the Nine Years' War (War of the League of Augsburg) signed by Louis, which established the *status quo ante bellum* (the existing state of things before the war). 
3. Age of Enlightenment—An intellectual movement (1715–1789) in France marked by the ideas of Voltaire, Montesquieu, and Rousseau, which challenged traditional authority and religion.
4. Bastille Day—Day when the people of Paris stormed the Bastille prison; national holiday of France
5. Reign of Terror (1793–1794)—A period during the French Revolution, led by **Maximilien Robespierre**, in which thousands of suspected 'enemies of the republic' were executed, often by guillotine.
6. Battle of Trafalgar—One of the greatest sea battles in history; the British navy defeated the combined French and Spanish navies
7. Battle of Leipzig—Called the Battle of Nations; combined forces of Europe defeated Napoleon's new army
8. Battle of Waterloo—Where Napoleon met his final defeat by allied troops under the command of the British **Duke of Wellington**. 
9. Congress of Vienna—Conference in Vienna, Austria, to redraw the map of Europe.
10. July Revolution (1830)—A revolution in France that forced **King Charles X** to abdicate and flee to England.
11. July Monarchy—Reign of Louis-Philippe in France
12. June Days—Rebellion which broke out among the common workers in France
13. Franco-Prussian War—War over who would succeed to the Spanish throne

## Concepts to Consider
1. Modern political ideas succeeded in England but failed in France because they were strengthened and restrained by the Christian heritage of the people. However, the only thing that can make modern political ideas workable in any country, biblical Christianity, had been driven out with the Huguenots.
2. French revolutionaries attempted to de-Christianize France by changing the seven-day week to ten days, renaming months, deleting all Christian holidays, and declaring the year 1792 to be **Year One**. Atheism contributed to the **Reign of Terror** by weakening traditional beliefs in God, morality, and the sacred value of human life.
3. The American War for Independence was a success because it was not a social movement of rights without acceptance of responsibility and because it was firmly grounded in principles, not merely selfish desires. The French Revolution failed because it embraced atheism. The aim of the French Revolution was initially to establish a government in the hands of the upper or middle classes, but it later became a movement to eliminate social classes and make all Frenchmen equal, in a sense, to make them all rulers of the country. The purpose for declaring independence was not equality but freedom.
