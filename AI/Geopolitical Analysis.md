## PAGE 1: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY AND INTRODUCTION

## Executive Summary

The strategic competition between the United States and China represents the single most consequential geopolitical force shaping global affairs in 2026. This rivalry transcends traditional bilateral disputes, fundamentally restructuring international trade networks, technological development pathways, alliance formations, and global security architectures. While the May 2026 Trump-Xi summit in Beijing achieved temporary de-escalation of trade tensions and established a framework for "strategic stability," deep-seated divergences over fundamental systemic issues remain unresolved and will persist throughout the long-term strategic horizon.

This comprehensive analysis examines the current state of US-China competition across multiple dimensions—trade, technology, military capability, and global influence—while providing detailed risk assessments, scenario planning frameworks, and policy recommendations for stakeholders ranging from government policymakers to multinational corporations.

## Critical Insight

Complete economic decoupling between the US and China remains unlikely despite accelerating reorientation. The relationship is evolving toward "managed competition" characterized by sustained high tariffs, technology restrictions, and supply chain diversification while maintaining essential economic linkages in non-sensitive sectors.

## 2026 as Tipping Point Year

Time Magazine's analysis of global risks identifies 2026 as a "tipping point year" for geopolitical uncertainty, with the United States positioned as the principal source of global risk due to its unwinding of the post-WWII global order it previously championed. This structural transformation creates unprecedented uncertainty for businesses, governments, and international institutions operating across US-China dividing lines.

## PAGE 3: TECHNOLOGICAL COMPETITION

## A. Semiconductor Export Controls: Timeline and Impact

**October 7, 2022: Initial Restrictions**  
The United States implemented unprecedented export controls targeting China's ability to access and develop advanced computing and semiconductor manufacturing items:

**Key Restrictions:**

- Limit AI chip access to Chinese entities
    
- Restrict Chinese design capability for advanced chips
    
- Stifle advanced chip manufacturing development
    
- Limit access to chip manufacturing technology
    

**Implementation Timeline:**

- October 12, 2022: Rulings affecting US persons assisting Chinese chip development without license
    
- October 21, 2022: All other rulings effective
    

**2023-2024 Expansion:**  
Restrictions were subsequently expanded in 2023 and 2024 to cover broader categories of advanced graphics processing units (GPUs) and semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

**March 2026: Draft New AI Chip Controls**  
The US government is drafting new export control regulations for AI chips that would:

- Cover most high-end processors sold by US companies
    
- Position US as "gatekeeper for global AI industry"
    
- Implement licensing based on computing power thresholds
    

**Export Threshold Framework (Proposed):**

|Export Size|Review Process|Conditions|
|---|---|---|
|Up to 1,000 GB300 GPUs|"Fairly simple review"|Exemptions available|
|1,000-200,000 GPUs|Stricter pre-clearance|Business model disclosure required|
|>200,000 GPUs|Host country government involvement|Allied countries only|

## B. Rare Earth Export Restrictions: China's Countermeasure

**April 2025: Announcement No. 18**  
China introduced new export licensing requirements for medium and heavy rare earth elements:

**Controlled Elements (7 of 17 rare earths):**

1. Samarium
    
2. Gadolinium
    
3. Terbium
    
4. Dysprosium
    
5. Lutetium
    
6. Scandium
    
7. Yttrium
    

**Coverage:** Controls extend beyond pure elements to encompass oxides, alloys, compounds, and mixtures.

**Extended Impact Beyond Technology/Defense:**

- **Automotive sector:** EV motors require heavy rare earths
    
- **Life sciences:** Medical imaging equipment
    
- **Aerospace:** Advanced propulsion systems
    
- **Lithium batteries:** Products containing Chinese rare earths require export license
    
- **Graphite & synthetic diamonds:** Now subject to export licensing
    

**November 8, 2025: New Magnet Rules**

- Any magnet containing trace amounts of Chinese rare earths requires Beijing's explicit approval
    
- Equipment/technology for processing rare earths and producing magnets prohibited from export
    
- Chinese firms banned from working with foreign companies on rare earths without permission
    

**Licensing Procedure:**

|License Type|Validity|Use Case|
|---|---|---|
|Individual License|Single shipment|Specific end-user|
|General License|Multiple shipments|Demonstrated compliance track record|

**Processing Time:** Up to 45 working days; delays possible for sensitive submissions

**National Security Impact:**  
China's 2025 rare earth restrictions exposed "critical US national security vulnerability." Washington responded with billions in domestic rare earth production investments.

## APPENDIX D: GLOSSARY

**Belt and Road Initiative (BRI):** China's international cooperation and economic strategy launched in 2013, comprising land-based Silk Road Economic Belt and maritime 21st Century Maritime Silk Road.

**China Plus One:** Strategy for firms to diversify supply chains across different regions rather than concentrating exclusively in China.

**Critical Minerals:** Natural resources essential for modern technology, defense, and energy systems (e.g., rare earths, lithium, cobalt).

**Geopolitical Distance:** Measure of how political tensions affect trade flows and economic linkages between countries.

**New Economic Nationalism:** 2026 trend characterized by prioritization of domestic economic capacity, trade restrictions, and technology sovereignty.

**PPP (Purchasing Power Parity):** Economic adjustment reflecting lower production costs in developing countries, making China's military spending ~60% of US rather than 30%.

**RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership):** Free trade agreement among 15 Asian nations promoting regional economic integration.

**Strategic Stability:** Framework for managing competition without escalation to direct conflict.

**Systemic Competition:** Fundamental rivalry over global order, governance models, and international institutions.
